The worked example · one public archive

Meet the texts
behind the demo.

What did Donald Trump talk about during his 2015–2016 campaign, and how did those themes appear during his first presidential term? TopicLLM uses his public Twitter posts to make a complex research process tangible.

The example begins with his June 16, 2015 campaign launch and ends with the selected archive on January 8, 2021. It contains 34,416 posting events. “Twitter” and “tweets” describe the platform during this period; it is now called X.

One collection. Two research questions.

A corpus is a collection of texts. Here, different dates give the same corpus two distinct jobs. The later posts are kept out of topic discovery, so the workflow can test whether campaign themes transfer to presidential communication.

What does one row represent?

One row is a posting event recorded for the account, with an identifier, timestamp and original text. Retweets and repeated texts under different IDs remain included. This captures what the account posted or amplified; it does not establish that Trump personally wrote every text or that the archive contains every post.

The source is CodeBreaker619’s Donald Trump Tweets Dataset on Kaggle. The raw text is preserved. A separate modeling copy removes specified formatting and common words so the software can compare language. Exact selection, preprocessing, attribution and hashes are in the data card.

From a topic to a result.

  1. Find topics. Software groups posts that use similar language. A group is a starting point for reading, not a finding by itself.
  2. Define themes. Two-pass human theme review interprets the original posts. Reviewers can combine several topics into one theme and write a codebook: definitions and rules for applying each theme.
  3. Check the measurement. Two independent human coding passes label sample posts. A separate human coder completes each pass; disagreements are adjudicated afterward. The agreed labels provide a reference for testing the classifier.

What happened in this example?

Human review retained 12 themes. The fixed classifier agreed with 70% of 250 held-out human reference labels. The completed teaching workflow then illustrates classification and aggregation across all 26,239 first-term posts.

A few terms, in plain language.

Topic and theme

A topic is a statistical group of similar language. A theme is a meaning defined through human interpretation. Several topics may support one theme.

Classifier and label

A classifier applies the codebook to a new post. A label is its assigned category. In this example, each post receives one primary theme or the residual category.

Development and held-out evaluation

Development is where a classifier is chosen. Held-out evaluation uses posts reserved from that decision to test the fixed classifier. Those evaluation labels cannot then be used for tuning while still being called an untouched test.

Residual and outlier

The residual label means none of the approved themes fits a post. An outlier is a post the discovery algorithm did not place in a topic. These occur at different stages and are not interchangeable.

Prevalence and model-assigned share

Prevalence is how common a theme is in a defined population. A model-assigned share is the fraction of posts given a label by a classifier. The demonstration reports the latter. Research use also requires meeting the declared evaluation criteria and human acceptance of the classifier’s errors.

Use the example to learn your own workflow.

The political archive is a familiar case for learning the method, not a claim about voters, public opinion or causal effects. The same workflow can be adapted to another text corpus with a new research question, human interpretation and independent validation.
